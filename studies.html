Studies

Closing the catalog, one problem a week

The catalog records what is open. This is the attempt to close some of it: one study a week, each aimed at a single entry, taken in priority order.

Scope is population-adjusted indirect comparison only, meaning MAIC, STC, ML-NMR, ML-UMR and NMI. Standard network meta-analysis is out of scope because it does not adjust for population differences, which is where these problems live.

Completed

ADJ-04 — Weighted conformal prediction keeps its marginal guarantee under shift by returning infinite intervals where the source has no data

Question. Does weighted conformal prediction give valid intervals for target patients under covariate shift, including where the source has no data?

Answer. No. Weighted conformal intervals kept marginal coverage over the target (0.904 to 0.919 at nominal 0.9), but beyond the source’s largest covariate value they covered 0.70 at a small shift and 0.83 at a moderate one, and at the largest shift they covered 0.99 only because 83% of those intervals were infinite. The method makes the support problem visible; it does not solve it. Estimating the density ratio changed nothing.

What it does not answer. Prediction intervals for individual outcomes rather than a marginal treatment effect; one covariate and one outcome model; no BART or Gaussian process arm; no support diagnostic scored as a classifier. Peer review has not been done.

ADJ-09 — An invariance screen for a bridging assumption, scored as a classifier

Question. Does instability across observed trials predict failure of a bridge used in a gap with no data, and does a screen on marginal effects fire under a valid bridge through non-collapsibility?

Answer. A stability screen across observed trials cannot confirm a bridge and refutes it rarely. With 4 or 10 trials, drift visible across them that biased the gap estimate by 0.28 was flagged in only 20% to 24% of analyses, and drift confined to the gap never above the null rate; passing the screen did not improve coverage of the reported analyses. Screening marginal log odds ratios raised false abstention under a valid bridge from 0.11 to 0.19 to 0.24 through non-collapsibility; screening conditional or standardized effects removed that.

What it does not answer. One event-rate level, no measurement shift, Cochran’s Q only; environments valid by construction. Peer review has not been done.

ADJ-10 — Modifier discovery across trials: no penalty is both stable and powerful, and study-level heterogeneity creates a false modifier that more trials entrench

Question. Are effect modifiers discovered from pooled trial data stable and correct enough to use, and do study-level confounders create false modifiers that more data do not remove?

Answer. No. Lasso discovery at the minimum-error penalty found the true modifiers but added 1.7 to 2.8 false ones among seven null covariates, and sets from independent datasets agreed with Jaccard similarity 0.37 to 0.57; the one-standard-error rule was stable only by selecting nothing (a single modifier found in 3% to 5% of analyses). With an unmeasured study-level cause of effect heterogeneity, pooled discovery selected the covariate whose trial means tracked it in 69% of analyses with 3 trials and 93% with 12 at the same total size, and raised false discoveries among the other covariates from 1.7 to 2.8 per analysis to 3.6 to 3.9; trial-specific treatment effects removed both.

What it does not answer. Lasso only (no multi-study causal forests); continuous outcome; linear modification; one overlap level; post-selection inference for the target not studied here. Peer review has not been done.

ADJ-15 — Target moments released under differential privacy

Question. When a target trial releases covariate moments under differential privacy, how much does ignoring the added noise cost MAIC coverage at realistic arm sizes and budgets, does propagating its known variance repair it, and when do extra released moments stop paying for their share of the budget?

Answer. Privacy noise is not negligible at usable budgets for trial-sized targets: at epsilon 1 and 150 patients it was 8% to 60% of the estimate’s variance and ignoring it cut coverage to 0.88 to 0.94; at 40 patients to 0.73. Its variance is known exactly, and adding it restored coverage in every scenario, conservatively where noise dominated. Releasing second moments pays only at larger budgets; at small ones it made MAIC infeasible in up to 87% of replicates.

What it does not answer. Laplace mechanism only; Gaussian mechanism, released correlations and overlap not varied. Results are conditional on feasible weights. Budget choice and governance are outside the study. Peer review has not been done.

ADJ-17 — An encoding that reconstructs 91% of the covariates’ variance dropped the effect modifier entirely

Question. Can a covariate encoding learned to reconstruct the covariates drop an effect modifier while reconstructing them well, and would a declared mapping or the full covariate set avoid the resulting transport bias?

Answer. No. With the treatment effect modified along a low-variance direction of 20 covariates, STC on five principal components reconstructed 91% of the covariate variance and lost the whole modification: bias -0.20 at a 0.5 SD target shift and -0.40 at 1 SD, coverage 0.49 and 0.02. Ten components (95% reconstructed) still lost most of it. STC on all covariates or on a declared modifier score was unbiased with nominal coverage in every cell, so reconstruction quality does not certify an encoding for transport.

What it does not answer. Principal components only; no treatment-effect-preserving or nonlinear encoder, nonlinear heterogeneity or measurement shift; continuous outcome. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-03 — An omitted component interaction biases never-administered regimens and leaks into regimens that do not contain it

Question. When additivity makes a never-administered combination estimable, how far can an omitted component interaction move its predicted effect, and does it contaminate other regimens?

Answer. No. At a synergy of 0.2 (twice a contrast’s SE), 15 of 32 never-administered regimen results that the additive model reported as estimable had 95% coverage below 0.90, down to 0.11. Synergy on a never co-administered pair went wholly into the regimens containing it and was undetectable; synergy on a co-administered pair was absorbed into the component effects and biased regimens without that pair by -0.125, including a never-administered one. Modeling co-administered pairs’ interactions removed the leakage. The share of a regimen’s pairs never co-administered did not flag the failures (AUROC 0.37).

What it does not answer. One connected network, continuous outcome, fixed effect; no disconnection, covariate adjustment, selection procedures or formal synergy bound. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-06 — Miscoding the shared component of a bridged network costs identifiability, not accuracy; miscoding an internal component biases the bridged estimate

Question. When the component coding of a disconnected network bridged by a shared component is wrong, does the error perturb the estimate or change what is estimable, and do sensitivity or probabilistic coding give honest intervals?

Answer. Partly. When the bridge is real, coding the shared component as a distinct agent removed that trial’s bridging information and, when it hit both bridging trials (up to 9.5% of replicates), made the target non-estimable, which a rank screen detects exactly; otherwise the estimate stayed unbiased with nominal coverage. The miscoding that left the row space intact, dropping an internal component, biased the target by about 0.8 SE and cut coverage to 0.83 given a miscoding. Probabilistic coding restored coverage (0.967 or more); coding sensitivity did too but was the whole line whenever a plausible coding broke the bridge.

What it does not answer. One hand-built network, fixed-effect contrast-level fits, no covariate adjustment, synergy or Bayesian arm; independent miscoding within a small plausible set. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-11 — Pooling standardized and unstandardized edges biases a component effect by exactly the untransported edges’ share of the population difference

Question. How large is the bias from pooling edges standardized to the target with edges left in their own populations, and is the two-stage route still the better practical choice than carrying every edge to the target?

Answer. No. The two-stage bias equaled the aggregate edges’ share of the pooled precision times the difference between their own-population and target effects (slope 0.97, R^2 0.997), zero only when modification or separation was zero. At 0.5 SD separation it reached -0.106 with coverage 0.27, and transporting every edge to the target had lower RMSE in all six such cells (0.038 to 0.089 against 0.047 to 0.113). Pooling conditional rather than marginal individual-data edges added up to 0.033 on the log odds ratio scale.

What it does not answer. Eight trials of one component rather than a component network; no one-stage component ML-NMR; aggregate covariate laws known; one network size. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-12 — Splitting a multi-arm trial’s adjusted contrasts is conservative for one contrast and anticonservative for another

Question. When a three-arm trial’s population-adjusted contrasts share an arm and a weight vector, what is lost by treating them as independent, is the error ever anticonservative, and is dropping the trial cheaper?

Answer. Ignoring the within-trial covariance errs in a direction set by the contrast, not by how the arms’ effect modification aligns. The trial’s own B-versus-C interval became conservative in every scenario, while the network A-versus-B interval, which borrows through the trial, fell below nominal in 15 of 30 scenarios. The shared-weighting part of the covariance took both signs but never reversed a contrast’s error. Dropping the trial widened the network interval by 29% to 91%.

What it does not answer. Continuous outcome, linear modification, one three-arm trial in a fixed two-trial aggregate network; four-arm trials, the one-stage ML-NMR comparator and a bootstrap are not run, and target moments are fixed. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-13 — Separating within-trial from across-trial component effect modification

Question. Component ML-NMR fits one shared interaction on a globally centered covariate, which algebraically forces the causal within-trial interaction to equal the confounded across-trial association. How much does that cost, when, and is the damage really caused by aggregate data as the catalog claims?

Answer. Fitting one shared interaction matrix on a globally centered covariate is not pooling: for a binary modifier with study prevalence p_s it is algebraically identical to imposing that the causal within-trial coefficient equals the confounded across-trial one. In the model simulated, coverage of a nominal 95% interval for the causal interaction falls from 93.8% when that happens to be true to 17.2% when the two are equal and opposite, and further in networks whose contrasts move two components at once. Separating them costs about 5% in interval width and removes an order of magnitude of bias. The catalog’s stated cause is incomplete: the loss is present with all twelve trials supplying individual data, so aggregate data are not necessary for it, but removing individual data roughly doubles the bias, so they amplify it.

What it does not answer. The registered global analysis is uninformative and the regime split that produced a usable answer was prompted by observing that failure, so it is not prespecified. The general headline about component ML-NMR is not established: the likelihood and nuisance structures are not mapped onto the fixed- and random-effect specifications the implemented method offers, and free study-by-treatment effects or priors can absorb or alter the term the identity isolates. A maximum-likelihood analogue of component ML-NMR. Implemented ML-NMR is Bayesian and priors matter most where interactions are weakly identified, so the coverage numbers do not transfer to multinma; the algebra does, and therefore so does the direction. One binary modifier, a log link, a Poisson outcome, additive components with no synergy, no random treatment-effect heterogeneity, no inconsistency, and exactly reported study prevalences. Under the discordance patterns the split model is correctly specified, so the study measures the cost of an algebraically known restriction rather than establishing that real networks contain within/across disagreement or how large it typically is. Continuous or multiple correlated modifiers, misspecified aggregate covariate distributions, and time-to-event outcomes are untested. It does not touch IDN-05.

CMP-14 — Prior-to-posterior contraction and effective likelihood rank measure information, not bias

Question. CMP-14 asks for two default summaries, prior-to-posterior contraction per interaction and an effective likelihood rank, so an analyst sees when an aggregate-only interaction is held up by its prior. Would those summaries tell the analyst whether the reported interval covers?

Answer. Not reliably, and the reason is structural. On an exact identity-link arm of 504 scenarios and a logit arm of 72, no threshold on contraction, on either effective-rank reading, or on the estimability screen separates scenarios whose 95% interval covers nominally from those that fail. The summaries are functions of Fisher information and the prior, so within one design they are exactly constant while coverage ranges from 0 to 0.96. They partly catch the failure they were designed for: contraction flags 59% of failures caused by a tight prior. They catch none of the 142 failures caused by a biased route, where the ecological route carries between-study confounding or the additivity route a synergy, because confounding moves the posterior mean and not its information. A clean contraction says the likelihood spoke, not that it was right. Every result is exploratory.

What it does not answer. Nothing here is confirmatory: E1 was computed before its protocol existed, E2’s separation rules were rebuilt after its output was read, and the cause decomposition was added for the manuscript. E1 is exact for a design realized at its quadrature nodes and E2 is asymptotic, so no posterior was sampled and neither is an average over realized designs. The exact invariance of the summaries holds for known-variance Gaussian models; with estimated variances or random effects they would move with a departure and whether that movement could ever warn is untested. One target component, one covariate, one network geometry, a normal within-study covariate law on the logit arm, and departures acting on the mean only. The protocol went through thirteen rounds of critique; the manuscript has not been peer reviewed.

CMP-15 — Calibrating the correlation does not fix the copula: the family moves a standardized contrast by up to 0.03 on the log odds and log hazard scales

Question. Once a Gaussian copula’s correlation is calibrated to the reported correlations, can the remaining error from copula family (asymmetry and tail dependence) move a target-standardized contrast?

Answer. Yes, modestly on the log odds and log hazard scales and strongly on the log rate scale. With margins and correlation matched, the calibrated Gaussian erred by up to 0.034 on the log odds ratio and 0.032 on the log cumulative hazard ratio (above 0.02 in 5 of 16 primary cells), with opposite signs for lower- and upper-tail dependence, and by up to 0.63 on the log rate ratio. The error plateaued by 256 integration points. A wrong flexible family was worse than the Gaussian in 61% of comparisons, so the defensible report is an envelope over families.

What it does not answer. Population-level; three exchangeable covariates with common margins; no mixed or discrete margins, vines or reconstruction uncertainty. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-16 — Sharing one heterogeneity parameter across a bridged network cost little; the interval method mattered more

Question. When two subnetworks of a bridged network have different between-study heterogeneity, does a shared tau give the bridged contrast valid intervals, and do stratified or shrunk alternatives do better at realistic study counts?

Answer. Largely yes for a contrast that sums both subnetworks. With one subnetwork five times as heterogeneous, shared, stratified and shrunk heterogeneity models covered within 0.016 of each other (shared 0.906 to 0.932 and stratified 0.917 to 0.943 across 3 to 12 studies per subnetwork), because a shared tau overstates one part’s variance and understates the other’s. The undercoverage came from ignoring uncertainty in tau: Hartung-Knapp intervals covered 0.97 to 0.997 at three studies, at about 1.5 times the width.

What it does not answer. A contrast drawing on one subnetwork only would carry the shared tau’s error without the offset; frequentist REML without priors; no residual-variance arm or IPD. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-18 — Adding time-constant component hazard ratios mispredicted a combination’s RMST when effects were delayed or follow-up differed

Question. When two components have different time profiles of effect and their trials differ in follow-up, does adding their time-constant hazard ratios predict the combination’s RMST, and does a piecewise extension fix it?

Answer. No. With both component effects delayed, adding time-constant hazard ratios overstated the combination’s 3-year RMST gain by 0.055 years (truth 0.405, coverage 0.88); a piecewise model reduced the bias to 0.017. With delayed and waning components and follow-up of 1.5 versus 3 years, the time-constant prediction was biased by -0.102 (coverage 0.82) and the piecewise model by -0.072 (coverage 0.91), because neither can learn a late effect the shorter trial never observed. With equal follow-up and effects summing to a constant, the time-constant model was accurate.

What it does not answer. Two single-component trials rather than a component network; no disconnection or population adjustment; one piecewise extension; one horizon. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-20 — The aggregate Poisson likelihood is biased by the exposure-rate covariance, and exposure-weighted covariate moments remove the bias

Question. The aggregate Poisson likelihood multiplies an arm’s total exposure by its mean modeled rate, which is exact only when exposure and rate are uncorrelated. The error is the factor 1 + rho CV(T) CV(lambda) per arm. How much bias does that put on a transported rate ratio at plausible levels of differential follow-up, and do exposure-weighted covariate summaries remove it?

Answer. No, the covariance cancels only when it is common to both arms. The bias followed the identity log{(1 + rho1 CV1(T) CV1(lambda)) / (1 + rho0 CV0(T) CV0(lambda))} (slope 0.994); at plausible dispersions with a differential correlation of at least 0.2, 27 of 64 cells had material bias or undercoverage, with bias up to 0.14 on the log rate ratio and coverage down to 0.17. Integrating aggregate arms over exposure-weighted covariate moments removed the bias everywhere (largest 0.008). Even with zero correlation the unweighted likelihood undercovered at large dispersions (0.84), because the finite-sample covariance is not zero.

What it does not answer. One covariate, one lognormal exposure law, no overdispersion, the same exposure mechanism in both studies; the likelihood is the study’s own code, not multinma. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-21 — An MNAR shift moved an anchored transported contrast in a direction that differed from dataset to dataset, but the tipping set was almost always one interval

Question. When an MNAR shift in imputed effect-modifier values enters a transported anchored contrast through several routes, is the contrast monotone in the shift, and is the tipping point a single value?

Answer. Partly. The tipping set was a single interval in at least 96% of replicates (non-single 3.0% to 4.4%, under the registered 5%), so a single tipping point sufficed here. But the routes offset only on average: the mean slope of the anchored contrast against the MNAR shift was -0.004 to 0.030, while per dataset its SD was 0.10 to 0.14 and it was positive in 48% to 62% of datasets. The direction in which an MNAR departure moves the contrast was not predictable from the design, so sensitivity analyses must explore both directions.

What it does not answer. Two anchored individual-data trials rather than a disconnected component network; one imputation per shift; congenial imputation; one MNAR mechanism; tipping set defined by the point estimate’s sign. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-22 — Which trials supply individual data, and what selection on it does

Question. When the trials that supply individual data are selected on observed trial variables or on their own effect modification, how biased is the interaction evidence, does combining with aggregate trials help, and can a leave-IPD-out diagnostic reveal it?

Answer. Selection of which trials supply individual data on an observed trial variable caused no bias, but selection on the trials’ own effect modification biased the within-trial interaction evidence by up to 0.14 (true 0.3), with coverage down to 0.59. Combining with the across-trial association in all trials halved the bias. A leave-IPD-out influence diagnostic did not respond to the selection, so the problem is invisible without a stated selection model.

What it does not answer. Pairwise continuous-outcome meta-analysis rather than component ML-NMR; no selection-model sensitivity analysis; one network size. Peer review has not been done.

CMP-24 — A diagonal-weight influence ranked studies correctly only with two-arm studies and heterogeneity held fixed

Question. Does the diagonal-weight influence that cpaic’s edge_influence() reports rank studies the way refitted leave-one-study-out references do, once multi-arm covariance and re-estimated heterogeneity enter?

Answer. No, the refuting sentence fails; the registered verdict is not fit for ranking. The diagonal influence equaled the correct hat row for two-arm networks (difference 8e-15) and ranked studies well there with tau fixed at 0 (mean Spearman with refitted variance importance 0.95 to 0.97; top study missed in 5% to 6%). With three-arm studies and tau fixed, Spearman stayed at 0.90 to 0.92 but the top study was missed in 28% to 46% of networks; in a post hoc rerun the diagonal’s choice was a three-arm study in almost all misses. With tau re-estimated, mean Spearman was 0.51 to 0.70, including two-arm networks, and the top study was missed in 20% to 59%.

What it does not answer. Standard network meta-analysis rather than component networks with population adjustment; study-level deletion; cpaic’s formula reimplemented, not called; no fixed-tau reference; a block-weighted hat matrix was not tested; 120 of 6000 networks with singular refits dropped. Peer review has not been done.

CMU-02 — Do prior-sensitivity diagnostics catch the analyses that are actually wrong?

Question. In a population-adjusted network a proper prior yields a proper posterior even where the likelihood identifies nothing, so a narrow interval can reflect the prior rather than the evidence. The diagnostics that would expose this exist. What are their operating characteristics: do they flag the analyses whose intervals actually miss the truth, and how often do they cry wolf?

Answer. At the thresholds the literature suggests, they miss most of it. Pairing each warning with whether that replicate’s own interval missed, a composite of four diagnostics has sensitivity 0.37 at a false-alarm rate of 0.16, falling to 0.13 outside two engineered geometries; the one sensitive rule fires on half the sound analyses too. Used as continuous scores the same statistics reach AUC 0.51 to 0.78, so this is a failure of thresholds rather than of the statistics. What no threshold repairs is that these diagnostics measure the prior’s influence while the harm comes from the prior’s location: with disconnected evidence and a tight prior four standard deviations from the truth, going from 100 to 400 per arm drops the warning rate from 0.98 to 0.01 while coverage stays near zero.

IDN-05 — A violation twice the size that would change a decision raises the shared effect modifier check’s alarm rate from 1.3% to 4.5%

Question. Population adjustment across a network assumes that a covariate modifies the effect of every active treatment in a class in the same way. There is one published within-network check on that assumption: fit the model with a single interaction shared across the class, refit with treatment-specific interactions one covariate at a time, and compare the posteriors and the model fit. Its authors say it likely has low power. Nobody has measured it, there is no threshold that separates a pass from a failure, and there is no statement of what a pass licenses. Scored against a known truth under a declared distribution of networks, what is it worth?

Answer. The check does not have the resolution to test the assumption it is read as testing. Against a violation already twice the size that would change a decision, the DIC rule fires in 0.045 [0.029, 0.070] of analyses, against 0.013 [0.005, 0.029] when the assumption holds exactly: a violation that doubles the error that matters moves the alarm rate by about three percentage points. The result does not depend on where the cut is put. Scored threshold-free, the DIC difference ranks a violated network above an intact one with an AUROC of 0.557 [0.517, 0.596] at that violation, so no threshold recovers a ranking the statistic does not contain. The posterior standard deviation of the interaction contrast runs 0.30 to 1.64 against the 0.153 that would make a transported estimate materially wrong, which is why. The equivalence reading, the only one that could confirm rather than merely fail to contradict, fired zero times in 3,200 opportunities, including every replicate where the assumption held exactly; that is a statement about precision, and partly about the prior, since independent normal(0, 2.5) priors already place 0.965 of the contrast beyond the margin. Following the check is still better than not: relaxing exactly what it fires on beats relaxing everything at every target displacement and beats imposing the restriction at every displacement except zero, where the two are indistinguishable.

What it does not answer. The design is a best case in several specific ways that favour the check, and failures attributable to those idealizations are conservative; no general lower-bound claim is made, because three features cut the other way. The true marginal reference risk in the target is supplied to the estimator. Covariates are normal with known target moments and study covariate means are drawn independently of study baselines and of the random treatment effects, so the ecological route identifying the aggregate-only treatment’s interaction is unbiased by construction. Conditional constancy holds and only one covariate drifts, linearly. Against that: the fits are fixed-effect while half the cells generate random treatment effects, which is a second specification error that inflates firing; the likelihood is integrated over 64 quadrature points and refitting at 256 changes the DIC verdict in 8.3% of a 24-network subset, which is noise belonging to the implementation and not to the evidence, and 256 points is not demonstrated to be converged; and a single individual-level study is an assumption about the network rather than necessarily a pessimistic one. Beyond that: what a second individual-level study would buy is not measured, that factor having been dropped to pay for treatment-effect heterogeneity. With two active treatments and two covariates, multiplicity across covariates and the selection involved in reading the widest of several pairwise differences are not measured. Where treatment-effect heterogeneity is present the estimand is not cleanly defined, since truth is set at a random effect of zero while a fixed-effect model targets a function of the realized studies; primary results are repeated on the sixteen homogeneous cells and do not change. The hierarchical middle ground between shared and independent interactions could not be fitted because the package does not implement it, and we did not implement it ourselves: that is a reason for its absence, not a justification. The five decision rules scored here are our formalizations of an exploratory published practice, not published rules, and their operating characteristics are ours. Fifty replicates per cell gives a per-cell standard error of 0.065, so nothing is read off a single cell.

MIS-03 — What conditioning on sampled target moments costs

Also bears on EST-07.

Question. Population adjustment treats a target trial’s published covariate means and standard deviations as exact, though they are sample estimates. By how much does that cost interval coverage at realistic sample sizes, and what would a publication have to report for a correction to work?

Answer. Two things, established by different means. Analytically: the variance a standard interval omits is (1-2k)Var_T(tau)/n_T to first order, where k is how far the target trial’s own treatment effect is modified by the same covariates as the source trial’s, so the sign is set by a quantity the usual inputs do not identify; and a correction adding only the positive term is closer to the right variance than doing nothing only when k < 1/4. Empirically, and only in an exploratory subset: the omission runs from 9.5% too much variance to 14.9% too little and moves coverage between 92.1% and 96.2%. The registered analysis is uninformative and the confirmatory coverage question remains open.

What it does not answer. The registered coverage question is not confirmatorily answered: every usable coverage result comes from a post-run restriction conditioning on an observed outcome. One estimator, one outcome type, one information structure. Covariates are multivariate normal, which is also the assumption the deployable correction is derived under, so it is measured under conditions favorable to it; skewed, bounded and categorical covariates are untested. The outcome is continuous and the effect additive, so marginal and conditional effects coincide, and nothing transfers directly to binary or time-to-event outcomes, where MAIC is most used. Effect modification lies exactly in the span of the matched moments, so this is a variance result under correct identification and says nothing about bias. The target moments and the target treatment effect come from the same trial, which is the common case but not the only one. STC, ML-NMR, ML-UMR and NMI have different sensitivities and are not covered. EST-07’s other components, model uncertainty in reconstructed correlations, inclusion-criteria ambiguity and secular drift, are not sampling error and are untouched. The alignment parameter imposes exact non-negative proportionality between source and target modifier coefficients, so modifier sets differing in direction or overlapping only partially are untested.

Question. Do one-at-a-time sensitivity analyses of population transportability and component invariance bound their joint effect on a cross-gap contrast, or can the layers offset and report spurious robustness?

Answer. No. With an omitted modifier (population layer) and component drift (bridge layer) acting on one cross-gap log odds ratio of -0.20, each one-at-a-time curve stayed within 0.16 and never reversed the decision, while the joint elicited box reached a bias of -0.41 and 7% of it reversed the decision; 23% to 26% of the box lay outside the one-at-a-time envelope at every scale. Component-by-modifier drift, which current tooling excludes, moved the estimate by 0.04 alone because it acts through its product with the modifier imbalance.

What it does not answer. Population-level calculation on one logistic model; no sampling, component network fit or survival scale; joint covariate law known, so the cost of eliciting it is not measured; component non-additivity and backbone interactions not included. Peer review has not been done.

QBA-23 — Bootstrap intervals over a bias grid when resamples fail

Question. When a bootstrap sensitivity analysis drops resamples whose weights fail near the edge of support, how does the interval behave, and is conditioning on success immaterial?

Answer. No. Near the edge of the source’s support, up to 67% of bootstrap resamples failed to produce MAIC weights, and the percentile interval from the survivors covered only 0.29 to 0.65 (0.14 to 0.56 counting failed original fits as giving no interval). The surviving resamples are those that contain the few patients who make the weights possible, so their spread understates the estimate’s. The failure share should be reported as a result and the interval withheld where it is material.

What it does not answer. One covariate, continuous outcome; cost and surrogate approaches and identified sets not studied. Peer review has not been done.

QBA-24 — Does a hazard-scale bias translate proportionally into RMST?

Question. Can a sensitivity analysis stated as a hazard-ratio bias be reinterpreted as a proportional bias in RMST, the quantity the decision uses?

Answer. No. The RMST change caused by a hazard-scale bias depends on the baseline hazard’s shape and the event level: at the same survival by 24 months the same bias moved RMST up to 1.44 times as much for a decreasing as for an increasing hazard, and the proportional (first-order) reinterpretation understated the change by about 25% with few events and overstated it by up to 8% with many. A hazard-ratio sensitivity analysis must be translated with the baseline curve or stated on the RMST scale.

What it does not answer. Only the hazard-to-RMST translation; the PAIC layer, pseudo-IPD and curve-based arms, reconstruction error and tipping sets not run. Peer review has not been done.

QBA-25 — Carrying a hazard-ratio bias into net benefit: the extrapolation family moved the decision more than the bias did

Question. Does propagating a bias-corrected hazard ratio and its interval into an economic model reproduce the decision uncertainty, or does the bias need to share a parameter space with the extrapolation model?

Answer. No. The probability of negative net benefit was 0.38 under point propagation of the reported hazard ratio and 0.67 when the bias (log hazard ratio within 0.15) and the treatment-effect extrapolation family were varied jointly. Almost all of the difference came from the extrapolation: a symmetric bias region alone moved the probability to 0.42, while a waning or cure extrapolation reversed the decision at the reported estimate. The worst joint combination (net benefit -9,618) was worse than any one-at-a-time scenario (-6,657).

What it does not answer. Deterministic survival curves instead of fitted extrapolations; three families, no splines; one symmetric bias parameter; costs and utilities fixed; one price. Peer review has not been done.

QBA-26 — A single-covariate benchmark against omitted composite structure

Question. Does the bias from dropping the strongest measured covariate bound the residual bias from several omitted variables, and does a scaled benchmark calibrate?

Answer. No. With three or six omitted variables each no stronger than the strongest measured covariate, the leave-one-covariate-out benchmark covered the true residual bias in at most 7% of analyses, the bias being 1.4 to 5.7 times the benchmark. Scaling by the square root of the number of omitted variables did not fix it; scaling by the number itself did when each omitted variable was weaker than the strongest measured one. The benchmark also shrank as omitted structure grew, through odds-ratio non-collapsibility.

What it does not answer. Omitted variables shifted in the same direction; one overlap level; negative-control route not run. Peer review has not been done.

SFW-10 — Stacking the weighting step into the MAIC sandwich makes intervals narrower, not better

Question. Does a stacked sandwich that accounts for weight estimation give better MAIC intervals than the fixed-weight sandwich or the ESS-based conventional variance, and what population does a trimmed MAIC actually estimate?

Answer. No. Stacking lowered the standard error in every untrimmed scenario (by up to 11%), because estimating calibration weights removes outcome variation the covariates explain; where MAIC intervals already undercover, at poor overlap, that made them worse (coverage down to 0.844). The fixed-weight sandwich was in band in 24 of 30 scenarios, the ESS-based variance in 18 and the stacked sandwich in 17, so current guidance stands. Trimming moved the estimand by up to 0.19 on the log odds ratio scale.

What it does not answer. No bootstrap and one sample size, so where stacking’s asymptotic advantage appears is not located. Binary outcome, three covariates, MAIC on means. Peer review has not been done.

SFW-12 — outstandR’s G-computation estimate moved by about 0.05 on the log odds ratio across defensible target reconstructions

SFW-14 — drMAIC 0.1.0 returns the MAIC estimate as its doubly robust estimate, with an interval six times too narrow

Question. Does drMAIC 0.1.0 implement a doubly robust MAIC with valid intervals: is its augmented estimate robust to a wrong weighting model when the outcome model is right, and do its intervals cover?

Answer. How a problem gets chosen

Not every open problem can be settled by a study, and the difference is not cosmetic. A simulation can measure bias, coverage and error rates, and it can show how badly a known nonidentification bites at realistic sample sizes. It cannot establish that a package is missing, that clinicians misread a plot, that a field under-reports something, or that a quantity is unidentifiable in principle. Those need software, a case study, a meta-research corpus, or a proof.

Every one of the 336 catalog entries was judged on both questions separately, by an external model, before the program started: is this a population-adjustment problem, and what kind of study would settle it. The queue below is the intersection.

What each study has to do

Simulation studies follow ADEMP: aims, data-generating mechanisms, estimands, methods, performance measures (Morris, White and Crowther 2019). Two rules follow from it and are enforced rather than encouraged.

Every performance measure carries a Monte Carlo standard error.
A bias of 0.02 is uninterpretable without knowing whether the Monte Carlo error is 0.001 or 0.03. The replicate count is derived in the protocol from a target Monte Carlo standard error, not chosen because it is a round number.
Failed replicates are results.
A method that converges on 60% of replicates and is unbiased on those is not an unbiased method. Convergence is reported for every method in every scenario.
The protocol is committed before the run.
It fixes the design and states in advance what result would count as showing the problem is real and what would count as showing it is not. The commit that adds it is the timestamp.

Code, protocols and results are at github.com/choxos/ITC-open-problems/tree/main/studies.
