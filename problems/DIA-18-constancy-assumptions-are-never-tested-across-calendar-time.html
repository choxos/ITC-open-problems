Constancy assumptions are never tested across calendar time

DIA-18 Confirmed open High priority Promising

Statement

Older-to-newer temporal holdout, fitting component and effect-modifier models on earlier trials and predicting a later one, is not performed in this literature, so there is no empirical estimate of how far constancy survives calendar-time change. What such a holdout tests is narrower than the source claims: it assesses joint out-of-time predictive transportability rather than equality of specific component or effect-modifier coefficients, and it requires the newer trial’s contrast to lie in the estimable row space of the training design with adequate covariate overlap. Prediction error against calendar gap also combines sampling error, ordinary between-study heterogeneity, design and outcome-definition changes and any genuine drift, so it does not by itself identify a decay rate or justify a universal trial-age cutoff. The claim that no published study has done this is an absence claim the literature auditor could not verify.

Why it is open

Constancy of component effects and effect modification is assumed across studies conducted decades apart, and calendar time bundles changes in standard of care, diagnostic criteria, outcome ascertainment and treatment versions, any of which can break it. Chronology is always recorded, so the holdout is uniquely feasible, yet it is not performed and no estimate of context-specific drift exists. Separating drift from sampling error, ordinary heterogeneity and design change needs repeated holdouts or a hierarchical temporal model rather than a single fit-and-predict exercise, which is more work than the design first appears to require.

What has been tried

No prior work is identified for this problem in the reviewed sources.

Probable solution or research direction

Run repeated older-to-newer holdouts on networks where the newer contrast is estimable from the earlier design and the estimands align, and report prediction error with its sources separated, using a hierarchical temporal model that distinguishes sampling error and ordinary between-study heterogeneity from systematic temporal drift. Report the results as context-specific drift estimates rather than a universal decay rate, and use them to inform drift priors in subnetwork-specific and hierarchical component models. Treat any inclusion-window recommendation as conditional on indication and outcome, since holdout prediction alone cannot justify a general trial-age cutoff.

Verification

Verdict. Confirmed open. Auditors agreed the problem is open and no counterevidence surfaced.

The source said. “Temporal validation, fitting component and effect-modifier models on older trials and predicting a newer trial to test explicitly whether those coefficients survive calendar-time change, has not been carried out, so there is no empirical estimate of how fast constancy decays.”

What was wrong with it. The source presented temporal holdout as a direct test of coefficient constancy that would yield a decay rate and justify inclusion windows; GPT-5.6 Sol narrowed it to a test of joint out-of-time predictive transportability whose error confounds sampling variation, heterogeneity and design change, so isolating drift needs repeated holdouts or a hierarchical temporal model. The literature auditor recorded the absence claim as unverified rather than confirmed, since no source asserts the absence.

Literature and prior-art check
open / supported-with-caveat. The mechanism claim is well supported by the wider meta-epidemiological literature, which documents that treatment effect estimates drift with calendar time: recursive cumulative meta-analyses show effects evolving over successive trials, and a meta-epidemiological survey of hundreds of meta-analyses reports that older randomized trials are associated with larger intervention effects. The logical claim, that fitting on older trials and predicting a newer one directly tests constancy of component and effect-modifier coefficients, is true by construction. The claim I cannot confirm is the absence one: ‘No published study tests whether component and effect-modifier constancy survives calendar-time change’ cannot be verified by failing to find one, and I found no source asserting the absence, so I mark that sub-claim unverified rather than confirmed. I retrieved the meta-epidemiological items at title and summary level only, which is why my confidence is at the abstract-reading level.
GPT-5.6 Sol, technical and source-code lens
open / overstated. C3 is technically plausible: calendar time can proxy changes in standard care, populations, measurement, and treatment versions. C1, however, overstates what temporal holdout prediction identifies. It tests joint out-of-time predictive transportability, not equality of specific component or effect-modifier coefficients. Prediction requires the newer trial’s component and interaction contrast to lie in the older training design’s estimable row space and requires adequate covariate overlap. Failure can arise from baseline-risk drift, outcome-definition changes, heterogeneity, or other misspecification; successful prediction does not prove coefficient constancy. Prediction error versus calendar gap also does not by itself identify a decay rate because it combines sampling error, ordinary heterogeneity, design changes, and potential temporal drift. Estimating drift requires repeated holdouts or a hierarchical temporal model that separates these sources. It cannot generally justify a universal trial-age cutoff. C2 is an uncited literature-absence assertion outside this audit. The work is a useful roadmap item, but not a uniquely decisive validation or an automatically High-severity omission.
